# The eleven o’clock test

Every business I’ve run has had some version of a piece of paper I was supposed to keep in my head. I owed somebody an answer. Something was waiting on me, or I was waiting on somebody else, and on Thursday I’d better remember which. Computers gave me more places to put that information. They didn’t necessarily relieve me of the job of keeping track of it.

At FlashForce, the company I’m building, we’re trying to make it possible for an ordinary business to hire an AI employee. By *employee* I mean software you can give a continuing responsibility to. There’s a perfectly reasonable objection to that choice of words: is this just a chatbot with a woman’s name? We decided to try a job inside our own business where I would know the difference. Her name is Carmen, and her assignment was to keep track of my open commitments and send me a private brief at eleven every weekday. What changed? What am I waiting on? Does somebody need an answer from me today?

I can get a chatbot to summarize an email while I’m looking at it. That isn’t a bad use of my time, but notice who found the email, decided it was important, and remembered to look in the first place. I did. The useful thing about handing this job to Carmen would be the freedom to spend Thursday morning thinking about something else. She’d have to know that Thursday was a workday, where she was allowed to look, what she’d told me on Wednesday and whether anything had happened since. Most of what I’d be paying for happens before the first sentence of the email.

One distinction became particularly important in our testing. We had an old notice from an outside organization. It was legitimate information about what had happened on the date the notice was sent. It wasn’t an answer to the question of where things stood today. That sounds fussy until you imagine running a shop and being told a part is on its way because somebody found a two-week-old shipment notification. Do you tell the customer to come in? Order the part somewhere else? The difference between “I checked today” and “this is the last word we have” is the whole reason you asked somebody to check.

When I gave feedback on the brief, what I wanted to see first was what happened and what happens next. Who owes the next move? Is there a deadline, or do we simply need to look again? Give me a way to get back to the actual work. Don’t make up an owner or a date to fill a blank on a form. I’d much rather have Carmen tell me that nobody has agreed to do something than have a nice, tidy report in which my name has quietly ended up next to it. The first one gives me a decision to make. The second commits me to a decision I haven’t made.

The harder part is what happens after a correction. Say I tell Carmen I’ve already answered one of the items, but we’re still waiting to hear back. Crossing it off entirely loses the follow-up. Leaving it on the list of things I owe means I have to explain myself again tomorrow. She needs to remember what changed about *whose turn it is*. A human colleague who knows what a follow-up is wouldn’t need me to dictate the database entry. That’s a reasonable standard to hold software to when you’ve chosen to call it an employee.

Of course remembering things on my behalf doesn’t entitle Carmen to know everything I know. Her assignment was to read the sources I gave her and write to me. It wasn’t to rummage through every account I own or reply to somebody in my name. On the other hand, when I’ve explicitly asked for a weekday email to me, stopping each morning to ask permission to send it would be ridiculous. People sometimes talk about human control as though the more often the machine interrupts you, the more control you have. I feel considerably more in control when I can give a clear instruction on Monday and trust it to stay in force on Tuesday. I want the interruption when the situation changes enough to need my judgment.

That’s also why names and personalities matter to me. Imagine a construction company with two AI employees, one who handles purchasing and another who deals with customers. The woman at the front desk shouldn’t need a diagram of our software to work out who knows whether the windows were ordered. She ought to know whom she works with and be able to send that colleague a message. Over time those two employees ought to be as easy to tell apart in a conversation as they are on the org chart. And Carmen at that construction company would have a completely different working life from Carmen at mine. Whatever she learned about their suppliers would be theirs. My ability to call someone Carmen wouldn’t buy me so much as a peek at their prices.

I got interested in this as a small-business owner, not because I had a particular affection for org charts. I’ve stood in the shop with the part in one hand and the phone in the other. In that situation the appeal of AI depends on whether I can hand something off, put the phone back in my pocket and carry on. I don’t want the job of designing a clever sequence of prompts. I already have a job. I suspect one of the big opportunities in this business is serving people who understand their own work extremely well and would prefer not to have to learn ours.

We’ve had an email arrive. We’ve also had work that I wouldn’t count as a useful day, and we’ve had to fix the system around Carmen. A delivered message answers one question: did something get to my inbox? It doesn’t tell me whether I made a better decision after reading it, or how much of my own time went into getting it there. This is an internal test, not a claim that somebody else can already depend on the result.

What I want to happen is fairly ordinary. One morning I’d like to see Carmen’s note, remember a commitment I would otherwise have missed, and go straight to the person who needs an answer. A week later I’d like to realize I’ve quit maintaining my own backup list. That second morning is the one that interests me most, because it’ll mean I’ve actually given away the job.
