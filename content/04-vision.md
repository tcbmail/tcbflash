# Talentless Doesn’t Mean Visionless

I heard a piece of music somebody had made with AI, and I liked it better than most of what I hear made the usual way. That was my entirely unscientific review as a guy listening to a song. What interested me afterward was the amount of trouble I’d have making anybody understand what I liked about it if I couldn’t just hit play.

I can’t sing it for you. I’m no good with an instrument, either, and I assure you that’s a finding I arrived at experimentally. But I have opinions about music. I can hear a voice that’s too polished for what the song is trying to say. I can like an arrangement right up until the strings come in. I don’t need to know the name of the chord to wish they’d held it a little longer. I suspect there are an awful lot of us in that particular band, none of us qualified to play in it.

Now imagine one of those people has a song in mind. Maybe all she has at first is a funny second verse about her brother and an idea of how it should feel when he hears it. Before she can let him hear the thing, she needs some combination of a voice, an instrument, recording equipment, money, and friends willing to give up a Saturday. Each of those is a perfectly reasonable requirement for a particular way of making music. Where I part company with some people is the idea that failing to meet those requirements tells us anything decisive about the second verse. Until she has a way to let us hear it, how the hell would we know?

I use the word *talentless* with a little affection and a fair amount of suspicion. There are skills I don’t have. Calling all of them “talent” turns a fairly specific observation about my hands or my voice into a grand verdict about what might be going on in my head. The reverse mistake is just as easy: liking a song doesn’t make me capable of writing one. I’m arguing for the right to find out, not an advance guarantee that I’m any good.

Imagine she gets a first version of the song back. It sounds competent. It’s also wrong: the singer takes the joke seriously, which is exactly the opposite of what makes it funny between her and her brother. She changes the words in the line leading up to it. She tries a quieter voice. The machine comes back with a pause she hadn’t thought of, and she keeps it. Somewhere in that exchange she discovers a better version than the one she thought she had in her head. I don’t see a need to pretend that she played the instruments. I also don’t see how you can take her out of that story and expect her brother to get the same song.

Software is the place where I know that feeling firsthand. Years ago a friend needed a working online store for his computer business. I knew enough to understand the job and had to teach myself SQL and ASP.NET to deliver it. It took months. That was a worthwhile education, but it also taught me to look at the price attached to an idea before I let myself get very interested in it. Could I build the whole thing myself? Could I afford the people who could? Quite often a sensible adult would stop right there. Being a sensible adult could get pretty damn depressing.

One of the projects I eventually did start was called Fishbrain. I was building software with an AI and running into an odd problem. We could spend an evening sorting out why a particular approach wouldn’t work. I’d come back later, eager to get on with the next part, and there I was explaining the bad idea all over again. I wanted a way to keep those decisions with the project so we could actually pick up where we’d left off. I could describe the aggravation in a sentence. There was a good deal more to making the thing I wanted. For once I felt as if I had enough help to take a serious run at it.

It didn’t take long for me to find a hole in my own idea. Suppose the system remembers something I told it while working on a different business. If it retrieves that fact in front of the wrong people, its excellent memory has just made it worse than useless. Now the question wasn’t simply how to remember. It was who was supposed to know what, and when remembering the right thing would be the wrong thing to do. I like that moment. I’d spent a long time wishing the machine could remember more. Getting close enough to build it taught me what I’d left out of the wish.

This is where I think the interesting argument about AI and creativity begins. There is a kind of work that happens *after* you can see or hear a first version. You notice what you meant, what you didn’t mean, what worked by accident, and what you’d change even though changing it means giving up the part that impressed everybody else. Somebody who couldn’t afford the first version didn’t get to do any of that work. Cheapening that first attempt does more than save the hours it used to cost. It admits a different set of people to the second attempt.

There’s a fair objection here. A musician who spent twenty years learning to make a living doing this may hear a business owner say “look what I can make now” and hear “look whom I don’t have to pay.” Sometimes that is precisely what it means. I don’t think admiring the new tool settles the musician’s claim to credit or a living. Nor do I think respect for the musician obliges the woman writing for her brother to leave the song unmade. In her case there probably wasn’t a commission waiting for a professional. The alternative was that he never heard the joke.

What I’d like is a little honesty about who did what and a great deal of curiosity about the result. A person can accept the first thing a machine offers and claim more credit than they deserve. A person can also have taste, make choices, reject easy answers, and spend months pursuing something that wouldn’t exist without them. I know from my own work that the difference can become painfully clear about five minutes after you put the result in somebody else’s hands.

When I first talked about Fishbrain and FlashForce, the best way I could explain the feeling was that my brain almost ached to get these things out. I’d like to earn a living at it, partly so I get to keep doing it. But before there was a company to explain to anybody, there was the relief of getting far enough to say, *Here. Try this. This is what I’ve been trying to tell you.* I heard a song somebody had made with an AI and recognized that feeling. I’m glad the person who made it got far enough to hit play.
