Current work

2023—present

Building a workplace for AI employees.

FlashForce is my primary focus: a system where a business can hire and manage AI employees with identity, roles, scoped memory, permissions, tools, authority, and workplace communication.

I’m less interested in another isolated assistant than I am in the management problem. What should an AI employee know? What is it allowed to do? Who gave it authority? How does its work become visible, reviewable, and useful to the rest of the company? Those are systems questions, and they feel very familiar.

Origin story

The career looks nonlinear.
The way of thinking isn’t.

I grew up in Ames, Iowa, in a lower-middle-class household. My father drove a truck as a Teamster; my mother worked as a USDA lab technician. I was intensely interested in computers before there was an obvious career identity attached to it.

01 Computers

Learning the whole machine

My first jobs were in cornfields, a restaurant, a garden center, a greenhouse, and a freight dock. Outside work, I spent as much time as I could around computers. At Rave Studios I helped where I could and learned around equipment that was unusually serious for the era: an SGI workstation, Avid editing, Pro Tools, sound booths, and large-scale VHS duplication.

I studied HTML, JavaScript, networking, Unix, Windows NT, routers, and hardware. In London in 1998, I worked at e-buy.com in software testing and NT administration. A senior programmer, David Wain, patiently showed me how to get below the surface of unfamiliar systems. Later that year, at the Iowa Energy Center, I handled nearly anything computer-related across three locations, including helping take one location from physical cabling to usable end-user computers. I also worked with then-new ISDN and Cisco networking.

The lesson I took from Keith Kutz was simple: point a capable person in the right direction, give them real responsibility and enough authority to do the work, then trust them.

02 Corporate tech

Citrix, competence, and fit

I found Citrix almost by accident at an Iowa State career fair. I already liked what its software could do: make limited hardware feel fast by moving the real work somewhere else. After an intense all-day interview in Florida, I joined as a test engineer in 2000.

I started with build verification, quickly answering whether a new engineering build installed and basically worked. I became more absorbed in ThinWire performance—tracking results, building graphs, working directly with developers, and becoming a point of contact for the testing effort. I loved being near engineers who really understood their systems.

Citrix also taught me that status, technical language, and actual competence do not always travel together. Ownership energized me; repetitive regression work and organizational busywork did not. A manager once discouraged me from moving from development and test into IT because it was considered a step down, even though I thought the work might fit me better. That was status substituting for fit. When layoffs approached, I volunteered through friends to be included, knowing the consequences would be harder for coworkers on work visas. The severance helped fund my first fixer-upper.

I still loved computers. I had simply learned that loving computers and wanting a conventional corporate technology career were not the same thing.

03 Physical world

Buildings have error messages, too

In 2003, while waiting for a fixer-upper to sell, I took on an e-commerce project for Dymin Systems. I did not yet have the database and application experience the job required, so I taught myself SQL and ASP.NET under deadline and delivered a working system. That confirmed I could learn a technical system from a standing start. It also confirmed that I preferred solving systems to grinding out code as a paid craft. Capital and access to other programmers—not a lack of interest in building systems—limited what Driven With Data could become as a conventional software company.

A temporary test-engineering role at Best Software in 2004 put me around Abra, an enterprise accounting and HR product. It was not a heroic career chapter. What stayed with me was a question: why did so much business administration require so much repetitive human labor? FlashForce was not conceived there; it is simply one of the earlier signals that makes more sense in hindsight.

I moved into construction and property operations on the Gulf Coast. Silver Hammer Construction and Little Pink Houses meant employees, trades, customers, vendors, materials, maintenance, and the physical cost of a bad decision. After Hurricane Katrina, that work included rebuilding damaged properties. The Waverly in Bay St. Louis—128 apartments—remains a project I am proud of.

I had seen the same failures repeat across rental properties, so I made choices for durability: long-lived cabinets and counters, durable floors, tile, and plumbing details meant to stop familiar leaks before they became familiar repairs. A building is a system. It is simply less polite when the system fails.

04 Operations

Software inside the work

After years of Gulf Coast construction and property intensity, I wanted a different life and business structure. A small used-car operation led me toward the stronger economics of used auto parts, where online marketplaces had demand for things that were difficult to identify and source. Ten Motors and Junkyarder became a long education in inventory, compatibility data, photography, listings, shipping, customer service, and e-commerce.

The ambition was to use software, catalog knowledge, and online distribution to compete with much larger salvage operations. Junkyarder was not a period away from technology. It was technology buried inside a physical operation, where data quality affected whether the right part reached a real customer.

05 Return

Back to computing, carrying the work with me

Modern AI made computing intellectually exciting to me again in a way it had not felt since the late 1990s and early 2000s. I did not spend the years outside professional software becoming a better software engineer. I spent them learning what work actually looks like: employees, customers, inventory, buildings, maintenance, deadlines, vendors, capital constraints, mistakes, and consequences.

I came back to computing carrying all of that with me.
