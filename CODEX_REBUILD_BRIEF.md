### Act 1: the computer kid

Ben grew up in Ames, Iowa in a lower-middle-class household. His father was a Teamster truck driver and his mother was a USDA lab technician. He was intensely interested in computers and technology before there was any obvious startup-founder identity attached to it.

Early jobs before professional technology included:
- 1990 — Asgrow: detasseling and rouging corn
- 1991 — Bonanza restaurant: dishwasher, busser, cleaning
- 1992–1994 — Kmart garden center: plants, customer assistance, loading mulch/soil/etc.
- 1996–1997 — Holub Greenhouse: greenhouse work, plants, customer assistance
- 1997 — Yellow Freight: dock worker

Formative technology exposure included spending time at **Rave Studios** as a teenager/young adult. It had unusually serious professional media equipment for the era, including an SGI O2 workstation, Avid editing, Pro Tools, sound booths, and large-scale VHS duplication equipment. Ben spent large amounts of time learning the equipment and doing small production/editing tasks. Do not claim Rave as a formal major career role unless the wording is clearly “spent time around / helped at / learned in.”

Technical interests included HTML, JavaScript, networking, Unix, Windows NT, routers, hardware, and later databases. He studied Cisco, Microsoft, and A+ certification material heavily but did not pursue/pay for the certification exams; **do not list these as credentials**.

### London / e-buy.com, 1998

At the University of North London period, Ben worked at early e-commerce company **e-buy.com** in software testing and Windows NT 4.0 administration.

A senior programmer there, David Wain, was an important early technical mentor. Ben remembers him as an unusually strong programmer and patient teacher who helped him understand difficult NT internals and other technology. Part of Ben’s work involved learning unfamiliar systems deeply, including studying barcode systems in the pre-QR era (UPC, ISBN, etc.).

This can be told briefly; it is about the habit of learning systems, not nostalgia for specific obsolete standards.

### Iowa Energy Center, 1998–1999

Network administrator for an organization of roughly 25–30 people spread across three locations. Ben handled essentially anything computer-related across Unix, Windows NT 4, and Mac systems. He helped fully set up one location from physical wire/cabling through usable end-user computers.

He remembers getting involved in then-new ISDN implementation and being particularly interested in Cisco routers/networking.

Keith Kutz was an exceptionally important boss/mentor. The management lesson Ben took from him was simple: point a capable person in the right direction, give meaningful responsibility and administrative authority, and trust them not to screw things up. This is a useful subtle precursor to Ben’s later thinking about managing AI employees. Do not turn it into a grand founding myth.

### Citrix, 2000–2003 — the key professional-tech origin chapter

This is one of the most important parts of the story.

Ben encountered Citrix almost accidentally at an Iowa State career fair. He knew the software through his girlfriend’s father, who sold Citrix products, and Ben already liked the core idea of making old/limited hardware feel fast through centralized computing.

After a telephone interview he was flown to Florida for an unusually intense all-day interview process and joined Citrix in Fort Lauderdale as a test engineer.

Products/technology:
- MetaFrame 1.8 / 2.0 era
- RDP / remote-desktop technology
- ThinWire performance work

Early on he was responsible for BVT/build verification testing after engineering builds. It involved quickly establishing that a build installed and basic functionality worked because a large engineering organization was waiting on the result.

He later became much more interested in ThinWire performance work. He worked closely with developers, tracked performance progress, created spreadsheets/graphs, and became a point of contact around the performance-testing effort. He especially enjoyed working with technically strong engineers, including a group colleagues informally referred to as the “Russian rocket scientists” after an acquired Israeli company’s engineers joined Citrix. Do not use that nickname if it reads awkwardly or culturally insensitive on the public site; the relevant fact is that he gravitated toward the strongest engineers regardless of organizational/social cliques.

The deeper Citrix lesson was not “everyone was incompetent.” Preserve nuance: Citrix exposed Ben to genuinely exceptional engineers **and** to the discovery that corporate status, money, technical-sounding language, and actual competence were not reliably the same thing.

He also became deeply frustrated by repetitive regression testing and organizational busywork: large surfaces being retested mechanically build after build, even when changes were narrow. Work that involved ownership of a real technical system energized him; procedural repetition did not.

A manager once discouraged Ben from transferring from development/test into IT because IT was considered a “step down” in the internal hierarchy, even though Ben thought the work might fit him better. That became an example of status substituting for fit.

When layoffs later approached, Ben was unhappy and knew coworkers on work visas could face much larger consequences if laid off. Through friends he effectively volunteered to be included in the cuts. He received a severance package and used part of it as seed money for his first fixer-upper property.

Citrix did not make Ben stop loving computers. It helped convince him that **loving computers and wanting a conventional corporate technology career were not the same thing**.

Do not include private medical events from this period.

### Driven With Data, 2003

Ben was financially stretched after buying a fixer-upper mobile home and needed income while waiting for it to sell.

His friend Billy needed an e-commerce site for **Dymin Systems**, a Des Moines-area business selling computers/parts and providing technical support.

Ben took on the job despite not yet having the full database/application skill set required, taught himself SQL and ASP.NET under deadline, and delivered a functioning e-commerce system in a matter of months.

The important lessons:
- he could learn and build a working technical system from a standing start
- he liked solving systems/problems more than grinding code as a paid craft
- the limiting factor in turning Driven With Data into a conventional software company was lack of capital/access to other programmers, not a desire to personally become a career coder

Do not use the private fee/estimated-value numbers publicly unless specifically needed; they are not needed.

### Best Software, 2004

Temporary test-engineering work in St. Petersburg, Florida, around **Abra**, enterprise accounting/HR software.

Ben does not regard this as a heroic career chapter. The useful observation is that the administrative machinery around enterprise accounting/HR reinforced a question that stayed with him: **why did so much business administration require so much repetitive human labor?**

Do not overstate this as “FlashForce was conceived in 2004.” It is an early signal in hindsight, not a direct founding event.

#### Silver Hammer Construction / Little Pink Houses, 2004–2009

Owner of construction/property businesses focused on building, remodeling, owning, and managing rental properties on the Gulf Coast.

The period included the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina and later the broader Gulf Coast recovery era. The construction company commonly had a meaningful crew and coordinated outside trades, but Ben does not want the public site to lean heavily on workforce/revenue bragging or large numeric claims.

A major project Ben remains proud of is **The Waverly in Bay St. Louis, Mississippi**, a 128-unit apartment property that had been heavily damaged by Katrina.

The useful systems story is durability: because Ben had personally seen how rental properties fail and how expensive recurring maintenance becomes, he made design/material choices around long-lived cabinets/counters, durable flooring, tile, and plumbing details intended to prevent the common under-sink leaks and damage he had repeatedly seen. The point is not interior-design vanity; it is systems thinking applied to buildings.

Do not publish disputed financial-return figures or claims about ownership percentages/partners. Do not mention former partners by name in negative contexts.

### Act 3: cars, parts, e-commerce and operations

Career chronology:

- 2010–2023 — Ten Motors / Junkyarder.com: online used auto-parts sales

The transition from construction to cars coincided with Ben wanting a different life and business structure after years of Gulf Coast property/construction intensity.

A small used-car operation led to discovering stronger economics in used auto parts. Online marketplaces, especially eBay, had demand for parts that were difficult to source. Junkyarder is important because it was not a rejection of technology. It was technology embedded inside a physical operation: vehicles, parts, inventory, compatibility, photography/listings, shipping, customer service and e-commerce.
