Who Gets the Hour?

In the used-parts business I could have a perfectly good piece of inventory that was, for all practical purposes, worthless. It was sitting on my shelf. The fellow who needed it was sitting somewhere with his car apart. Neither of us knew the other one existed. Somebody had to identify the part, photograph it, work out what else it fit, price it and put it online. Until somebody did, my excellent inventory was a heavy object taking up space.

Consider what happens when software gets good enough to do an hour of that work. In my case the first hour it saves might be mine. I still take the pictures. I still check anything that looks questionable, and when the part sells I’m still the shipping department. But I could list parts I otherwise wouldn’t have gotten around to. The buyer gets his car fixed. I make a sale. Or maybe I sell the same number of parts and go fishing a little earlier. As the owner of that particular hour, I’m delighted by either outcome.

Now picture the shop down the road. It already has somebody good at listings. Same shelf, same part, same buyer, same piece of software. The owner there has several choices. He might put his employee on the stack of returns nobody’s had time to sort out. He might list twice as much inventory. He might also decide he only needs that employee three days a week. There’s no technical feature that determines which of those things he’ll do. The software can work exactly as advertised in all three cases. Ask the employee whether they’re the same outcome.

This isn’t an abstract argument for me. I’m building FlashForce because I want a small company to have access to help it couldn’t otherwise afford. I believe that’s worth doing. I also stand to benefit if it works. When somebody whose job might be affected asks what I think is going to happen to them, it won’t do to answer with a story about a different business where a person got to go fishing. I’d be picking the case in which I happen to be both the employer and the beneficiary and passing it off as the whole story.

I come at this with a fairly strong instinct to leave people alone. I like that you can notice an opportunity other people have missed, take a risk on it, and keep a worthwhile share of what you make. I wouldn’t want to live in a system that sent me to a committee to explain why a used-parts yard needed a better way to list taillights. I doubt the committee would be especially excited to see me, either. Prices and the prospect of earning money can get people to do useful, unglamorous things for strangers they’ll never meet. I’ve made my living that way. Nothing about AI has made me want to give that up.

What it has changed is what I’m willing to conclude about the person who can no longer sell an hour of their time. Suppose our listings employee taught the software how to tell two similar parts apart. On Friday that knowledge was valuable enough for the business to pay them. On Monday the program can apply it to the next hundred listings without their help. Their judgment didn’t deteriorate over the weekend. In fact, the better they were at explaining the difference, the less reason there may be to keep paying for it. I have a hard time getting from there to the conclusion that the person has become any less deserving of a place to live.

The objection I take seriously is that somebody still has to do the work. A building needs a roof; somebody has to put it on. People who take the trouble to learn that trade, show up in the heat and get it right ought to be paid well for it. I’m not proposing to send them and everybody who stayed home the same check at the end of the week. There’s an enormous amount of room between paying everybody the same and deciding that the person who couldn’t find a buyer for their labor should have nowhere to sleep. The possibility that some people will do less than I’d like is a real cost. So is turning a parent who can’t find enough paid hours into somebody who can’t buy groceries.

There’s a question about inheritance that I can’t quite get past. Imagine two people who do no paid work this year. One can afford it because his parents left him money. The other can’t. I don’t particularly care to march into the first fellow’s house and demand a timesheet. It’s his life. But notice how quickly the conversation about the second fellow turns into a judgment of his character. Maybe he’s looking after someone. Maybe he’s trying to start something. Maybe he really would prefer to sit on his ass. That last choice isn’t one I’d advise. I’m still having trouble explaining why it should cost one man his home and the other man precisely nothing. The fact that one of them chose his parents more intelligently is not an argument I can make with a straight face.

Where I’ve come down is that I want markets and I want a floor under people. By a floor I mean the fairly literal things a person needs: food, a place to live, access to medical care. Above that floor I want plenty of room for ambition, competition, luck, and the rewards of building something people want. I used to think those two instincts pulled hard in opposite directions. Now I wonder how much worthwhile risk we miss out on because people who have ideas can’t afford to be wrong even once. I know what it feels like to want the chance to try an idea. Having the freedom to start a business and having the means to survive its first bad month are two different things.

I don’t know the best way to pay for that floor. Sending money doesn’t build a missing apartment. And nothing about an AI getting better at office work tells us what a house, a meal or a visit to the doctor will cost a particular family. Those are reasons to argue seriously about what would work, including what an experiment costs and who it leaves out. They’re not much of a reason to assume the arrangement we had before the experiment is morally correct. Nor do I need to know the year the last job disappears. For the person whose hours were cut this week, the question has already arrived.

I’m not going to put the software away out of nostalgia for typing part numbers. I plan to use it. I also won’t tell the person who spent Friday teaching it that their problem on Monday is a failure to adapt. As far as I can see, they adapted just fine. What hasn’t caught up is our arrangement for paying them once we can keep using what they know without having to buy another hour of their time. I want that person to get something more substantial out of the future than a thank-you for helping build it.
